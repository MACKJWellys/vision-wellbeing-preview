Research

I maintain an active research involvement at the University of Edinburgh. Both projects below reflect the same principle I bring to clinical work: therapy should be structured, practical, and focused on what actually helps.

Current research projects

Lead researcher

Behavioural Activation in NHS Scotland

In preparation for publication

A qualitative investigation into how brief behavioural activation can be delivered effectively within NHS Scotland services — what helps and what gets in the way of real-world implementation.

Background

Behavioural activation is one of the most effective brief interventions for low mood and depression. This project examines how it translates from controlled research settings into the realities of NHS Scotland primary care, where time, resources, and workforce constraints shape what’s possible.

Methodology

Qualitative interviews with practitioners delivering brief behavioural activation across NHS Scotland services, analysed using thematic analysis to identify facilitators and barriers to effective delivery.

Research assistant

Single-Session Intervention for Parents

Pilot study

A pilot study evaluating whether a single-session CBT-informed intervention can support parents of young children with anxiety, giving families practical tools without requiring a full course of therapy.

Rationale

Waiting lists for child and adolescent mental health services are long. Parents often want to help but don’t know how. This project tests whether a single structured session can give parents enough to make a meaningful difference while they wait — or as a standalone intervention for milder presentations.

Why research matters to clinical practice

My research keeps me closely engaged with what the evidence actually shows — not just what worked in a textbook, but what works in practice, with real people, in real services. That shapes the therapy I offer: structured, practical, and informed by what the data says rather than habit or fashion.

It also means I can be transparent with clients about what’s well-supported, what’s promising, and what’s still uncertain. I think that honesty is part of good clinical practice.
